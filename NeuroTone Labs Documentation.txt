1. MENTAL HEALTH PREDICTION: RELEVANCE TO THE THEME
Health prediction is a very important task in healthcare analytics and affective computing. Predicting health status from behavioural and physiological data is important for early support, personalised care and better health outcomes. This process requires expert knowledge, verified datasets and sufficient computing resources to turn raw data into accurate predictions.
Experts and healthcare workers usually assess health status through interviews, questionnaires or expensive systems. These traditional methods can take a lot of time, may be subjective and are not always accessible to groups, startups and people in developing areas.
Our idea uses the mental_health_multimodal dataset, which contains scores (Depression_Score, Anxiety_Score and Stress_Score) and 18 behavioural and physiological features. These features capture signs from user interactions, facial expressions, voice and body signals. We use them to build a tool that helps researchers, developers and healthcare professionals classify mental health status.
The tool uses a 1D Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to analyse the features and predict a person's health status. It identifies four categories: Healthy, Mild_Stress, Moderate_Stress and Severe_Stress. The MENTAL HEALTH PREDICTION system aims to apply AI to healthcare analytics and mental health monitoring.
This approach may reduce the computing resources needed for initial assessments and shorten the time required to identify patterns in data. It can help improve mental health monitoring systems, healthcare decision-support tools and user experience design.

 4. BUSINESS BACKGROUND
As people become more interested in health and well-being, understanding mental health through behavioural and physiological data has become increasingly valuable. However, many researchers and organisations do not have access to affordable mental health assessment tools. Some existing systems are expensive, require specialised hardware or use proprietary technology. These limitations, together with limited computing resources, make it difficult to apply mental health prediction in practical situations.
At NEUROTONE LABS, we recognise these challenges in healthcare analytics and mental health monitoring. We therefore developed an AI-based tool that analyses structured behavioural and physiological features to classify mental health status. This approach aims to reduce some of the barriers to accessing assessment technology.
By using machine learning, MENTAL HEALTH PREDICTION generates classification results and confidence scores to support researchers and developers in analysing data. The system is intended to support research and decision-making rather than replace professional mental health assessments or medical diagnoses.

C. Model
MENTAL HEALTH PREDICTION uses a 1D Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) implemented with TensorFlow/Keras. The model is trained from scratch rather than using a pretrained model.
The architecture combines convolutional layers, pooling layers, dropout regularisation and dense layers to classify the input data.
Architecture details:
•	Input: 21 features, reshaped into the format required by a 1D CNN.
•	Convolutional Layers: Multiple Conv1D layers with ReLU activation to learn patterns from the input features.
•	Batch Normalisation: BatchNormalization layers to support more stable training.
•	Pooling Layers: MaxPooling1D layers to reduce the size of intermediate feature representations.
•	Dropout: Dropout layers with rates between 0.3 and 0.5 to help reduce overfitting.
•	Dense Layers: Fully connected layers to combine learned features and perform classification.
•	Output: A dense layer with four neurons, one for each mental health status category, using Softmax activation to produce class probabilities.
•	Optimiser: Adam with a learning rate of 0.001.
•	Loss Function: Categorical cross-entropy for multi-class classification.
•	Callbacks: Early stopping with a patience of 10 epochs and model checkpointing to save the best model.

G. EXPERIMENTAL ANALYSIS
After training and testing the 1D CNN model, the project identified several important considerations.
•	Model Performance: The CNN was designed to learn from all 21 input features and classify data into four mental health categories. Actual performance should be reported using the recorded evaluation metrics.
•	Feature Integration: The model combines Depression_Score, Anxiety_Score and Stress_Score with 18 behavioural and physiological features.
•	Class Imbalance: Severe_Stress represents only 3.2% of the dataset. This small number of samples may make it harder for the model to recognise this category accurately.
•	Preprocessing Impact: Imputation and standardisation help prepare the data for training and ensure that missing values and different feature scales are handled consistently.
•	Deep Learning Benefits: The 1D CNN can learn feature patterns automatically, reducing the need for manual feature engineering.
Reference: experiment_report.md in mental_health_21_feature_cnn_experiment/.
These findings demonstrate how a 1D CNN can be applied to mental health status classification using structured features. The mental_health_cnn.py script combines training and prediction functionality and automatically generates evaluation files and reports.
The model's effectiveness must be determined from the actual test results, particularly its precision, recall and F1-score for Severe_Stress. Overall accuracy alone may not provide a complete picture because the dataset is imbalanced.
H. ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS
The use of AI to predict mental health status raises several ethical concerns that must be considered.
•	Clinical Validation: The model must not be used to diagnose mental health conditions without appropriate clinical evaluation and validation by qualified healthcare professionals.
•	Fairness: The model should be evaluated to identify potential bias and differences in performance across relevant groups.
•	Transparency: Users must understand the system's purpose, limitations and experimental status.
•	Privacy: Behavioural and physiological information can be sensitive. Data must be handled securely, with appropriate access controls and consent where required.
•	Explainability: Users should be provided with understandable information about the model's predictions and, where possible, the features influencing its outputs.
•	Responsible Use: Confidence scores must not be treated as proof that a prediction is medically correct. The system should support research and decision-making rather than replace professional judgement.
•	Regulatory Compliance: Any real-world use must comply with applicable healthcare requirements and data protection laws, including relevant South African legislation.
